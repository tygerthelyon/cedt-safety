Suspected Radiation Exposure
In any case of known or suspected exposure to ionizing radiation, immediately notify your supervisor and contact the Health Physics Department at ext. 24226 (or Campus Safety at 88 after hours). Do not wait to confirm the exposure was significant before reporting; Health Physics will assess the situation.

McMaster's Radiation Safety Program is governed by RMM 700: Radiation Safety Program for University Laboratories, and RMM 701: X-Ray Safety Program. The University delegates authority for radiation safety compliance to the Health Physics Department and the Health Physics Advisory Committee (HPAC), who jointly administer the radiation safety program and publish supporting guidance documents.

Radiation is broadly characterized as ionizing or non-ionizing, based on whether it carries enough energy to displace electrons from atoms or molecules.

Ionizing Radiation

Ionizing radiation, including alpha and beta particles, and gamma, X-ray, and UV photons, carries enough energy to cause structural damage in living tissue. At sufficient doses, this damage can impair or destroy organ function. Ontario sets annual exposure limits for the general public; these vary by the part of the body exposed (whole body, eye lens, individual organs, hands/feet), and Health Physics monitors all radiation work on campus to keep doses well below these limits and as low as reasonably achievable (the "ALARA" principle).

Individuals conducting radioactive work in a typical McMaster research lab generally receive an annual dose far below regulatory limits, often comparable to a single chest X-ray per year. Dose monitoring (e.g., dosimeters) is provided by Health Physics where required.

Radioactive Isotopes

Nuclear substances are controlled federally by the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission (CNSC). Radioisotopes carry both direct radiation dose risk and contamination risk during storage, handling, and disposal.

  • All personnel working with radioactive materials must complete Health Physics' training and be classified accordingly (e.g., as a Nuclear Energy Worker, if applicable).
  • Any lab storing or using radioactive material must hold a valid permit, issued by the Health Physics Advisory Committee following a lab assessment.
  • Warning signs must be posted in any area where radioactive materials are stored or used.
  • No food or drink is permitted in labs housing radioactive materials.
  • Additional precautions apply for pregnant workers in radiation areas; consult Health Physics directly.

X-Ray Radiation

X-ray safety in Ontario is legislated under O. Reg. 861, and McMaster's program is detailed in RMM 701. Access to X-ray-generating equipment is restricted to personnel who have completed Health Physics' X-ray Safety Training. CEDT's X-ray systems incorporate interlocked shielding and fail-safe indicators designed to make operator exposure effectively impossible under normal use; systems are surveyed by Health Physics when commissioned, with ongoing periodic inspections by CEDT supervisory staff to confirm continued safe operation.

Non-Ionizing Electromagnetic Radiation

Non-ionizing radiation — visible and infrared light, microwaves, and radio waves — does not carry enough energy to directly damage atoms or molecules, but sufficiently intense exposure can still cause thermal (heat-related) tissue damage. Most sources lose intensity rapidly with distance; shielding with a grounded conductive mesh (with openings smaller than one quarter of the radiation's wavelength) is an effective control where needed.

Particular caution applies to microwave and radiofrequency generators used in plasma-generation systems common in CEDT cleanroom processes. These can produce high-power, directional fields capable of causing tissue damage if shielding is inadequate.
